Mathematical Discourse

My dissertation focused on the use of discourse in elementary mathematics. Discourse is vital to the development of mathematical thinking and understanding. Productive mathematical discourse is something that must be taught and nurtured in the classroom. Students need to be understand that while there are correct answers in math, the are multiple ways in which a problem can be solved. Students need to learn how to explain their mathematical thinking in a way that other can understand. The development of these explanations deepens their understanding, builds connections in mathematical concepts, and supports the development of a mathematical community.

When students are encouraged to discuss their thinking and share their ideas, they are working together to learn and grow. All ideas are welcome and misconceptions are seen as opportunities for growth and new learning. Productive mathematical discourse does not happen by chance—it must be intentionally taught and nurtured in the classroom. While there may be correct answers in mathematics, there are often multiple valid approaches to solving a problem. Students must learn to articulate their mathematical reasoning in ways that others can understand, a skill that deepens their own understanding, strengthens conceptual connections, and fosters a sense of mathematical community.

Early Childhood Education

As a former kindergarten through second grade teacher, I am deeply committed to advocating for developmentally appropriate early childhood education. These early years are the foundation for a child’s academic, social, and emotional growth, yet recent shifts toward teacher-led, direct instruction often fail to meet the unique needs of young learners.

Children are natural explorers, problem-solvers, and creators. They thrive in environments where they can engage in hands-on experiences, meaningful exploration, and abundant opportunities to move, talk, and play. These experiences are not just enjoyable—they are the most effective way for young children to make sense of the world. Play and exploration allow them to develop fine and gross motor skills, practice social interactions, build language, and strengthen cognitive connections. In this way, play is not separate from learning; it is learning.

When young children are encouraged to connect new ideas to what they already know, discuss their thinking, and work together, they are building the skills that will serve them for a lifetime. By creating joyful, active, and developmentally appropriate learning environments, we ensure that children not only succeed in school but also carry a deep love of learning into the future.

We must let children be children and honor the way they learn best.

"Children learn as they play. More importantly, in play, children learn how to learn." —Fred Donaldson

Teacher Professional Development

Professional development must go beyond a one-time workshop or a generic overview—it should be ongoing, relevant, and directly connected to the realities of a teacher’s classroom. The most impactful learning experiences are grounded in the needs of students and responsive to the everyday challenges educators face. Meaningful professional development equips teachers with strategies and tools they can immediately implement, sparking reflection, offering practical takeaways, and inspiring new approaches without adding to their workload.

Equally important, professional learning should be collaborative and situated to the classroom. Teachers thrive when they can share ideas, ask questions, and learn from one another. Additionally, they are inspired when they see how their learning can impact the students in their classroom. When professional development is approached as a continuous, shared journey related to the needs and interest of the classroom, rather than an isolated event, it not only strengthens teaching practice but also fosters a more connected and supportive school community—ultimately benefiting every student.
